\[W^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix}0.4&-0.3\\0.2&0.5\\-0.6&0.1\end{bmatrix},\; b^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix}0.1\\-0.2\end{bmatrix},\quad W^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}0.5&-0.4\\0.7&0.3\end{bmatrix},\; b^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}0.05\\-0.1\end{bmatrix}\]

(a) Hidden layer activations

Pre-activation: \(z^{(1)} = W^{(1)T}x + b^{(1)}\). Each hidden unit takes a weighted sum of all three inputs (column \(j\) of \(W^{(1)}\) holds the weights into hidden unit \(j\)):

\[z^{(1)}_1 = (0.4)(0.6) + (0.2)(0.9) + (-0.6)(0.4) + 0.1 = 0.24 + 0.18 - 0.24 + 0.1 = 0.28\]

\[z^{(1)}_2 = (-0.3)(0.6) + (0.5)(0.9) + (0.1)(0.4) - 0.2 = -0.18 + 0.45 + 0.04 - 0.2 = 0.11\]

ReLU: \(h_j=\max(0, z^{(1)}_j)\). Both pre-activations are positive, so they pass unchanged.

Answer: \(z^{(1)}=[0.28,\;0.11]^T\), hence \(h=[0.28,\;0.11]^T\).

(b) Output layer activations

\[\hat y_1 = \frac{1}{1+e^{-0.146}} = 0.5364,\qquad \hat y_2 = \frac{1}{1+e^{-0.129}} = 0.5322\]

Answer: \(\hat y = [0.5364,\;0.5322]^T\).

(c) Categorical cross-entropy loss, target \(y=[1,0]^T\)

\(L = -\sum_i y_i \ln \hat y_i = -\big(1\cdot\ln 0.5364 + 0\cdot \ln 0.5322\big) = -\ln(0.5364)\)

(a) LSTM cell diagram

The LSTM cell (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997) contains three sigmoid gates regulating a memory cell \(C_t\) that runs through the cell with only element-wise interactions:

  • Forget gate \(f_t=\sigma(W_f^{(h)}h_{t-1}+W_f^{(x)}x_t+b_f)\) — decides what fraction of \(C_{t-1}\) is retained.
  • Input gate \(i_t=\sigma(\cdot)\) with candidate \(\tilde C_t=\tanh(\cdot)\) — decides what new information is written.
  • Cell update \(C_t = f_t\odot C_{t-1} + i_t\odot \tilde C_t\) (the additive path that mitigates vanishing gradients).
  • Output gate \(o_t=\sigma(\cdot)\), exposing \(h_t = o_t\odot\tanh(C_t)\).
h_{t-1}, x_t → [σ forget f_t] · [σ input i_t] · [tanh candidate C̃_t] · [σ output o_t]
                 ↓
        C_t = f_t⊙C_{t-1} + i_t⊙C̃_t
                 ↓ tanh, gated by o_t
        h_t = o_t ⊙ tanh(C_t)

Question 1 — Gradient Descent & Hyperparameter Tuning 5 + 5 + 4

(a) Gradient descent in backpropagation; one problem

Training a network means finding weights \(\theta\) that minimize a loss \(L(\theta)\). Backpropagation applies the chain rule to compute the gradient \(\nabla_\theta L\) of the loss with respect to every weight and bias, layer by layer, from the output backwards. Gradient descent then uses this gradient as the direction of steepest local decrease and iteratively updates each parameter:

\[\theta^{(k+1)} = \theta^{(k)} - \eta\,\nabla_\theta L(\theta^{(k)})\]

where \(\eta\) is the learning rate. Repeating this over many mini-batches moves the weights toward a (local) minimum of the loss surface, which corresponds to the "optimal" weights the network can find.

Problem: the loss surface of a neural network is non-convex, so gradient descent can become trapped in local minima or saddle points / flat plateaus, and in deep networks the repeated multiplication of derivatives causes vanishing (or exploding) gradients, making early layers learn extremely slowly (Bengio et al., 1994). Convergence is also sensitive to the choice of learning rate.

(b) Learning rate too high vs. too low

The learning rate \(\alpha\) scales the step \(\Delta w = -\alpha\,\partial L/\partial w\) taken at every iteration.

Too high: the update overshoots the minimum. The loss oscillates or diverges — the weights bounce across the valley walls and can grow without bound (numerically exploding to NaN). Training becomes unstable and may never converge, or converges to a poor, noisy solution.

Too low: each step is tiny, so convergence is extremely slow; training may effectively stall on plateaus, get stuck in the nearest shallow local minimum, and consume excessive compute/epochs before reaching an acceptable loss.

Summary: too high → oscillation/divergence; too low → very slow convergence and risk of stalling. A well-chosen (often decayed/scheduled) rate balances the two.

(c) Random vs. grid search; "coarse to fine"

Grid search evaluates hyperparameters on a fixed lattice; with \(n\) values per hyperparameter and \(k\) hyperparameters it needs \(n^k\) trials, yet only tries \(n\) distinct values of each individual hyperparameter. In practice only a few hyperparameters matter strongly (e.g., learning rate), so most grid trials waste budget re-testing the same value of the important hyperparameter while varying unimportant ones. Random search samples every trial from the full ranges, so the same budget explores many more distinct values of every hyperparameter — Bergstra & Bengio (2012) showed empirically and theoretically that random search finds equal or better configurations in far fewer trials.

Coarse-to-fine strategy: first run a coarse random search over broad ranges (often on a logarithmic scale, e.g., \(\alpha \in [10^{-6},10^{0}]\)). Identify the region where the best-performing samples cluster. Then zoom in: define a narrower range around that region and sample more densely, possibly repeating the process. This concentrates the sampling budget where good configurations live, refining the estimate of the optimum progressively.

Question 3 — Universal Approximation, Bias–Variance, L2 Regularization 4 + 5 + 5

(a) Universal Approximation Theorem; are shallow networks preferable?

Statement: A feed-forward network with a single hidden layer containing a finite (but sufficiently large) number of units with a non-linear activation (sigmoidal in Cybenko, 1989; any non-polynomial in Hornik, 1991 / Leshno et al., 1993) can approximate any continuous function on a compact subset of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) to arbitrary accuracy.

Does this imply shallow is preferable? No. The theorem is an existence result: it guarantees an approximator exists but says nothing about (i) how many hidden units are needed — for many function classes the required width grows exponentially with input dimension, whereas depth achieves the same accuracy with exponentially fewer units (Montúfar et al., 2014; Telgarsky, 2016); (ii) whether gradient-based learning can find those weights; or (iii) generalization. Deep architectures also learn hierarchical, reusable feature representations (edges → parts → objects), which is why deep networks dominate empirically in vision and language.

(b) Training error 2%, Dev error 15% — diagnosis and two remedies

The gap between training (2%) and dev (15%) error is large while training error itself is low. Low training error → low bias; large train–dev gap → the model fits the training data far better than unseen data.

Diagnosis: high variance (overfitting), not high bias.

Two practical steps: (1) Get more training data (or use data augmentation) — the most reliable variance reducer. (2) Add regularization — L2 weight decay, dropout (Srivastava et al., 2014), or early stopping — to constrain model capacity.

(c) L2-regularized logistic regression cost; effect of \(\lambda\)

\[J(w,b) = -\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big[y^{(i)}\ln h_w(x^{(i)}) + (1-y^{(i)})\ln\big(1-h_w(x^{(i)})\big)\Big] + \frac{\lambda}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j^2\]

\(\lambda\) increases → weights shrink → simpler, smoother decision boundary → model complexity decreases, training error increases (bias grows); if too large, underfitting. \(\lambda \to 0\) → no penalty → training error decreases but variance/overfitting risk grows. \(\lambda\) thus traverses the bias–variance trade-off; an intermediate value minimizes dev error.

Question 5 — Regularization, Vanishing Gradient, Augmentation, Multi-crop, Parameter Counting 4+2+2+2+2+2

(a) Regularization; three techniques

Regularization is any modification to the learning algorithm intended to reduce generalization (test) error without necessarily reducing training error (Goodfellow et al., 2016).

  • L2 weight decay: adds \(\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert w\rVert_2^2\) to the loss, shrinking weights toward zero and producing smoother functions.
  • Dropout: randomly zeroes each hidden unit with probability \(p\) during training (Srivastava et al., 2014); prevents co-adaptation and approximates an ensemble.
  • Early stopping: monitor validation loss and stop when it starts increasing — limits effective capacity.

(b) Deep or shallow — which is more prone to vanishing gradients?

Deep networks. Backpropagation multiplies local derivatives across layers: \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w^{(1)}} \propto \prod_{l} \sigma'(z^{(l)})\,W^{(l)}\). With saturating activations \(|\sigma'|\le 0.25\), a product over many layers shrinks exponentially with depth (Bengio et al., 1994; Glorot & Bengio, 2010).

(c) Which activation functions are prone, and why?

Sigmoid and tanh. The sigmoid's derivative \(\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\) has maximum 0.25 and tends to 0 for large \(|z|\); tanh's derivative \(1-\tanh^2(z)\) also saturates to 0. ReLU avoids this on its active side (derivative exactly 1).

(d) Should data augmentation be applied to the test set?

No. Augmentation is a training-time regularizer. The test set's role is to provide an unbiased estimate of performance on the true data distribution; transforming it changes that distribution and makes the reported metric non-comparable. (Test-time augmentation such as multi-crop evaluation is a distinct inference averaging procedure, and must be reported explicitly.)

(e) Multi-crop testing

At evaluation time, several deterministic crops of each test image are taken — classically 10-crop: the four corners plus the center, and their horizontal mirrors (Krizhevsky et al., 2012) — each is passed through the network, and the softmax outputs are averaged. It reduces prediction variance (a small ensemble), compensates for off-center objects, and typically yields a consistent accuracy gain at extra inference cost.

(a) DenseNet vs ResNet connectivity; which preserves features better?

ResNet (He et al., 2016): identity skip connections added element-wise: \(x_{l+1} = x_l + F(x_l)\). Earlier feature maps are merged into the sum and not individually accessible downstream. Question 7 — Non-Max Suppression & IoU 2 + 2 + 2 + 8

(a) Which problem does NMS address?

Detectors output many overlapping boxes for the same object. NMS removes these duplicate/redundant detections, keeping only the highest-confidence box per object so that each object is reported once.

(b) NMS with multiple object classes

NMS is applied independently per class: detections are grouped by predicted class; within each class boxes are sorted by confidence and suppression runs using IoU only among boxes of that same class. Boxes of different classes never suppress each other.

(c) Two conditions where anchor-based frameworks degrade

  • Objects whose scale or aspect ratio is poorly covered by the anchor priors (very small, very large, extremely elongated) — no anchor achieves sufficient IoU with the ground truth, so recall drops.
  • Dense scenes of heavily overlapping objects of the same class — nearby instances compete for the same anchors, and NMS suppresses correct neighbouring detections.

(d) IoU computation and NMS (IoU threshold 0.5)

(i) IoU(A,B): intersection \([40,90]\times[40,90]=2500\); union \(=3600+3600-2500=4700\). IoU = 2500/4700 = 0.532

(ii) IoU(A,C): intersection \(2500\); union \(=3600+2500-2500=3600\). IoU = 2500/3600 = 0.694

(iii) IoU(A,D): no overlap. IoU = 0

(iv) Sort: A(0.88) > B(0.80) > C(0.72) > D(0.60). Select A; suppress B (0.532>0.5) and C (0.694>0.5); D survives. Select D. Remaining: {A, D}.

(v) At threshold 0.6: B (0.532 ≤ 0.6) is kept; C (0.694 > 0.6) suppressed; D kept. Final set: {A, B, D}. Raising the threshold makes NMS more permissive — boxes must overlap more strongly to be considered duplicates.

Output per axis: \(O = \frac{32-5+0}{1}+1 = 28\); depth = 2 → 28 × 28 × 2. Parameters: weights \(5\cdot5\cdot3\cdot2 = 150\), biases 2 → 152. (Parameter count is independent of padding and stride.)

Question 5 — Flattened FC Layer vs Full-size Conv Filters 5 marks

The two options are mathematically equivalent: (a) FC: \(100\times5+5 = 505\); (b) a 10×10 filter on a 10×10 input (valid) yields a 1×1 output per filter; \(5\times(10\cdot10\cdot1+1)=505\). When the kernel covers the entire input, convolution degenerates to a global dot product — identical expressive power and parameter count; all convolutional advantages (locality, weight sharing, equivariance) are forfeited. Either is acceptable; choose (a) as simpler — or, if convolutional benefits are desired, use small filters (e.g., 3×3).

Parameters \(= F(K\times K\times C_{in}) + F\). (i) True — weights proportional to input depth. (ii) True — one bias per filter. (iii) False — stride affects output size only. (iv) False — padding affects output size only. (i) and (ii) are true; (iii) and (iv) false.

Answer: (iv) None of the above.

Data augmentation, dropout (Srivastava et al., 2014) and early stopping are all regularizers; since each prevents overfitting, the technique that does not is "none of the above."

Question 1 — Fitting, Perceptron Learning, Features from Digit Data 4 + 4 + 6

(a) Underfitting, overfitting, bias–variance trade-off

Underfitting: model too simple — high error on train and test (high bias). Overfitting: model fits noise — low train error, high test error (high variance). Trade-off: \(\mathbb{E}[(y-\hat f)^2] = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \sigma^2_{noise}\); capacity ↑ ⇒ bias ↓, variance ↑; optimum at intermediate capacity (Hastie et al., 2009).

(b) Perceptron learning rule — factors and parameters

The perceptron (Rosenblatt, 1958) computes \(O = f(\sum_i w_i x_i + \theta)\); update \(w_i \leftarrow w_i + LR\cdot I_i\cdot Err\) with \(Err=T-O\). Factors: weights (0.3, 0.5, −0.4) and threshold t = 0.0 (decision boundary/bias); error sign gives correction direction; input \(I_i\) assigns credit only to active inputs; learning rate LR scales steps (large → oscillation, small → slow); epochs continue until an error-free epoch (perceptron convergence theorem for linearly separable data).

(c) Features from handwritten digits; input and hidden layer operation

Expected hierarchy: oriented edges/stroke segments → mid-level curves, loops/holes (0,6,8,9), endpoints, junctions (4,8) → digit-discriminative configurations (loop+tail → 9; two loops → 8), as visualized in LeCun et al. (1989) on this ZIP data. Input layer: one unit per pixel (e.g., 16×16 = 256 normalized intensities), no computation. Hidden layers: \(h_j = \phi(\sum_i w_{ji}x_i + b_j)\); each weight vector is a learned template; deeper layers combine detections into abstract, spatially tolerant features until the output layer separates the ten classes.

Question 3 — Conv vs FC, 1×1 Convolutions, Residual Connections, EfficientNet 2 + 4 + 4 + 4

(a) Advantages of convolutional layers

Sparse local connectivity (small receptive fields match image structure); weight sharing (same filter everywhere ⇒ far fewer parameters, translation equivariance).

(b) 1×1 convolutions as dimension reduction

A 1×1 conv with \(F\) filters is a per-pixel linear map \(\mathbb{R}^{C_{in}}\to\mathbb{R}^{F}\) across channels (plus nonlinearity). Example: 5×5 conv on 28×28×192 with 32 filters ≈ 120M multiplications; inserting a 1×1 bottleneck to 16 channels: ≈2.4M + ≈10M ≈ 12.4M — ~10× reduction (Szegedy et al., 2015). Cost scales with \(C_{in}\), so reducing channels by r cuts the large conv's cost by r.

(c) Residual connections F(x)+x

The block learns the residual \(F(x)=H(x)-x\). Identity is trivial (drive weights to zero), addressing the degradation problem; backprop gives \(\partial(F(x)+x)/\partial x = \partial F/\partial x + I\), an un-attenuated gradient path (He et al., 2016). Plain stacking lacks both, so it saturates and degrades.

(d) EfficientNet

Compound scaling of depth, width, resolution with one coefficient \(\phi\): \(d=\alpha^\phi, w=\beta^\phi, r=\gamma^\phi\) s.t. \(\alpha\beta^2\gamma^2\approx2\); each increment of \(\phi\) ≈ doubles FLOPs while balancing capacity; B0→B7 trace a superior accuracy-per-FLOP frontier (Tan & Le, 2019).

Question 4 — R-CNN Disadvantage, Faster R-CNN Pipeline, One-stage vs Two-stage 4 + 5 + 5

(a) Primary disadvantage of R-CNN

Extreme slowness/redundancy: full CNN per ~2,000 proposals, no shared computation (~47 s/image), fragmented multi-stage training with feature caching.

(b) Faster R-CNN step by step

  • Stage 1: shared convolutional backbone → shared feature map; Region Proposal Network slides over it scoring k anchors (objectness + box regression); top proposals kept after NMS.
  • Stage 2: RoI pooling crops each proposal to fixed size from the shared map; detection head classifies (softmax + background) and regresses class-specific boxes; low-confidence discarded and per-class NMS gives final detections.

Both stages share backbone features and train jointly end-to-end (Ren et al., 2015).

(c) One-stage vs two-stage

(i) One-stage (YOLO, SSD, RetinaNet): single dense pass, real-time 30–150+ FPS, historically less accurate on small objects (foreground–background imbalance; focal loss). Two-stage (Faster R-CNN): slower (~5–20 FPS), more accurate, better localization. (ii) One-stage for latency-critical (driving, video, mobile); two-stage for accuracy-critical, latency-tolerant (medical, satellite, annotation). (iii) Two-stage: proposal → per-region features → per-region head; one-stage: dense anchors/points classified and regressed in one shot over feature pyramids.

Question 5 — Generative Adversarial Networks 4 + 6 + 4

(a) What are GANs?

Two networks in a minimax game (Goodfellow et al., 2014): generator \(G\) maps noise \(z\) to synthetic samples; discriminator \(D\) outputs the probability an input is real. At equilibrium \(p_g = p_{data}\) and \(D = 1/2\) everywhere.

(b) Why the generator's cost is the opposite

\[\min_G \max_D V = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{data}}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z}[\log(1-D(G(z)))]\]

\(D\) maximizes both terms; \(G\) controls and minimizes the second. Zero-sum: \(J^{(G)} = -J^{(D)}\); every improvement of \(D\) raises \(G\)'s loss, forcing better samples. (Practically the non-saturating \(\max_G \log D(G(z))\) is used.)

(c) Discriminator too strong

\(D(G(z))\approx 0\) ⇒ \(\log(1-D(G(z)))\) saturates ⇒ vanishing generator gradient ⇒ training stalls (D loss → 0, G loss high/static), possible mode collapse. Remedies: non-saturating loss, slower D updates, noise/label smoothing, WGAN-GP (Arjovsky et al., 2017).

Question 6 — Multi-Head Attention, LSTM Diagram and Components 4 + 5 + 5

(a) Why multi-head attention?

Single head = one weighted-average relevance pattern per position. Multi-head (Vaswani et al., 2017) projects Q, K, V into h subspaces and runs attention in parallel: heads specialize (syntax, coreference, adjacency, long-range semantics), attend to different positions simultaneously, and each works at \(d_k = d_{model}/h\) so total cost matches one full head.

(b) LSTM diagram

Inputs h_{t-1}, x_t feed all four blocks in parallel:
 f_t = σ(W_f[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_f) · i_t = σ(W_i[·]+b_i) · C̃_t = tanh(W_c[·]+b_c) · o_t = σ(W_o[·]+b_o)
   ↓ element-wise on the cell-state highway

Activations: gates sigmoid ([0,1] soft switches); candidate and output squashing tanh ([−1,1]).

(c) Purpose of each component

  • Cell state \(C_t\): long-term memory conveyor belt; additive updates give gradients an almost unimpeded path (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997).
  • Forget gate: fraction of \(C_{t-1}\) retained per dimension.
  • Input gate: which candidate components are written.
  • Candidate \(\tilde C_t\): proposed new content, bounded by tanh.
  • Output gate: which parts of memory are exposed.
  • Hidden state \(h_t\): working output to next step and upper layers.

Question 7 — Seq2seq, the Fixed Context Bottleneck, and Attention 3 + 3 + 8

(a) Encoder–decoder seq2seq

Encoder RNN reads the input token by token; its final hidden/cell state is the fixed-size context vector. Decoder is initialized with it and generates autoregressively from the previous token and its own state until end-of-sequence (Sutskever et al., 2014; Cho et al., 2014).

(b) Fixed-context limitation

Information bottleneck: the whole sequence compressed into one vector; early tokens overwritten; quality degrades sharply with length; long gradient paths hamper training.

(c) Attention

(i) Encoder keeps all hidden states \(h_1,\dots,h_T\); an attention module lets the decoder look back at them at every step; the static context is replaced by a dynamic per-step one, adding gradient shortcuts to every input position (Bahdanau et al., 2015). (ii) At step t: scores \(e_{t,i} = \text{score}(s_{t-1}, h_i)\) (additive or dot-product); weights \(\alpha_{t,i} = \exp(e_{t,i})/\sum_j \exp(e_{t,j})\); context \(c_t = \sum_i \alpha_{t,i} h_i\) — recomputed per step, softly selecting relevant input tokens; learned end-to-end; generalized to self-attention in the Transformer.

M7 — mAP procedure and precision/recall operating point (Final 2023, Q2c) 8

Question. Dog test set; confidence thresholds [0.99, 0.95, 0.90, 0.5, 0.10]; IoU 0.5 matching. (i) How is mAP calculated? (ii) At one threshold Precision = 1/1 = 1.0 and Recall = 1/3 = 0.33 — what does this say?

Solution. (i) Match detections at IoU ≥ 0.5 (TP/FP; unmatched GT = FN); sort by confidence; sweep thresholds computing P = TP/(TP+FP), R = TP/(TP+FN); build the (interpolated) precision–recall curve; AP = area under it; mAP = mean AP over classes. (ii) 1 detection, correct; 3 dogs present ⇒ 2 missed. A very conservative operating point: no false alarms but poor coverage — top-left of the PR curve; lower the threshold if misses are costly.

M8 — 1×1 convolution bottleneck arithmetic (Final 2023, Q3b) 4

Question. Give the mathematical reasoning for 1×1 convolutions as dimension reduction before 3×3/5×5 convolutions in Inception, and the effect on computational complexity.

Solution. A 1×1 conv with F filters is a per-pixel linear map across channels, \(\mathbb{R}^{C_{in}}\to\mathbb{R}^{F}\); cost of a conv scales as \(n_H n_W n_c' \cdot f^2 n_c\), i.e. linearly in input depth. Example: 5×5, 32 filters on 28×28×192 = \(28^2\cdot32\cdot25\cdot192 \approx 120\)M mults; with a 1×1 bottleneck to 16 channels: \(28^2\cdot16\cdot192 \approx 2.4\)M + \(28^2\cdot32\cdot25\cdot16 \approx 10\)M ≈ 12.4M — about a 10× reduction for identical input/output shapes, plus an extra nonlinearity.

Chapter 7: Trajectory Generation — Joint-Space Schemes

Cubic polynomials · Via points · Higher-order (quintic) polynomials · Linear function with parabolic blends

Preliminary — What a Joint-Space Scheme Is Doing

A trajectory generator manufactures a smooth \(\theta(t)\) honouring constraints on position/velocity. Counting argument: degree-\(n\) polynomial has \(n+1\) coefficients ⇒ four constraints (positions + velocities at both ends) ⇒ cubic; six constraints ⇒ quintic; two cubics spliced at a via point carry eight coefficients, the extra two coming from velocity and acceleration continuity across the splice.

1. Cubic Polynomials (Rest-to-Rest Motion)

Constraints \(\theta(0)=\theta_0,\ \dot\theta(0)=0,\ \theta(t_f)=\theta_f,\ \dot\theta(t_f)=0\) with \(\theta(t)=a_0+a_1t+a_2t^2+a_3t^3\) give:

\[a_0=\theta_0,\quad a_1=0,\quad a_2=\frac{3(\theta_f-\theta_0)}{t_f^2},\quad a_3=-\frac{2(\theta_f-\theta_0)}{t_f^3}\]

Sign check: for forward motion \(a_2>0, a_3<0\) — built-in accelerate-then-brake structure; \(a_2=-\tfrac32 a_3 t_f\).

Example 1 (Slide 16)

Rest at 15° → 75° in 3 s: \(a_0=15, a_1=0, a_2=\frac{3}{9}(60)=20, a_3=-\frac{2}{27}(60)=-4.44\). \(\theta(t)=15+20t^2-4.44t^3\); \(\dot\theta=40t-13.32t^2\); \(\ddot\theta=40-26.64t\). Check \(\theta(3)=75\) ✓, \(\dot\theta(3)\approx0\) ✓.

2. Cubic with Non-Zero End Velocities

\[a_0=\theta_0,\quad a_1=\dot\theta_0,\quad a_2=\frac{3}{t_f^2}(\theta_f-\theta_0)-\frac{2\dot\theta_0}{t_f}-\frac{\dot\theta_f}{t_f},\quad a_3=-\frac{2}{t_f^3}(\theta_f-\theta_0)+\frac{1}{t_f^2}(\dot\theta_f+\dot\theta_0)\]

Setting \(\dot\theta_0=\dot\theta_f=0\) collapses to the rest-to-rest coefficients.

Exercise 2 (Slide 20)

\(\theta_0=5,\ \theta_v=15,\ \theta_g=40\), 1 s per segment, via velocity 17.5°/s. Segment 1: \(a_{10}=5, a_{11}=0, a_{12}=12.5, a_{13}=-2.5\) ⇒ \(\theta_1=5+12.5t^2-2.5t^3\). Segment 2: \(a_{20}=15, a_{21}=17.5, a_{22}=40, a_{23}=-32.5\) ⇒ \(\theta_2=15+17.5t+40t^2-32.5t^3\). Checks: \(\theta_1(1)=15,\ \dot\theta_1(1)=17.5,\ \theta_2(1)=40,\ \dot\theta_2(1)=0\) ✓. Acceleration jumps 10 → 80 °/s² at the via (velocity continuity only) — motivation for the continuous-acceleration spline.

3. Cubic Polynomials for a Path with Via Points (continuous acceleration)

Two cubics, local clocks; eight constraints: positions at start/via/goal (twice at via), rest at both ends, velocity and acceleration continuity at the via. For equal durations \(t_f\):

\[a_{10}=\theta_0,\ a_{11}=0,\ a_{12}=\frac{12\theta_v-3\theta_g-9\theta_0}{4t_f^2},\ a_{13}=\frac{-8\theta_v+3\theta_g+5\theta_0}{4t_f^3}\]

\[a_{20}=\theta_v,\ a_{21}=\frac{3\theta_g-3\theta_0}{4t_f},\ a_{22}=\frac{-12\theta_v+6\theta_g+6\theta_0}{4t_f^2},\ a_{23}=\frac{8\theta_v-5\theta_g-3\theta_0}{4t_f^3}\]

Note \(a_{21}\) (via velocity) depends only on the endpoints, not the via point.

Exercise 3 (Slide 25)

\(\theta_0=5,\ \theta_v=15,\ \theta_g=-10\), \(t_f=2\): Segment 1: \(a_{12}=10.3125, a_{13}=-3.9063\) ⇒ \(\theta_1=5+10.3125t^2-3.9063t^3\). Segment 2: \(a_{21}=-5.625, a_{22}=-13.125, a_{23}=4.8438\) ⇒ \(\theta_2=15-5.625t-13.125t^2+4.8438t^3\). All splice conditions verified: \(\theta_1(2)=15\), \(\dot\theta_1(2)=-5.625=\dot\theta_2(0)\), \(\ddot\theta_1(2)=-26.25=\ddot\theta_2(0)\), \(\theta_2(2)=-10\), \(\dot\theta_2(2)=0\) ✓.

4. Higher-Order Polynomial: The Quintic

Six constraints (position, velocity, acceleration at both ends) ⇒

\[a_0=\theta_0,\quad a_1=\dot\theta_0,\quad a_2=\frac{\ddot\theta_0}{2}\]

\[a_3=\frac{20\theta_f-20\theta_0-(8\dot\theta_f+12\dot\theta_0)t_f-(3\ddot\theta_0-\ddot\theta_f)t_f^2}{2t_f^3}\]

\[a_4=\frac{30\theta_0-30\theta_f+(14\dot\theta_f+16\dot\theta_0)t_f+(3\ddot\theta_0-2\ddot\theta_f)t_f^2}{2t_f^4}\]

\[a_5=\frac{12\theta_f-12\theta_0-(6\dot\theta_f+6\dot\theta_0)t_f-(\ddot\theta_0-\ddot\theta_f)t_f^2}{2t_f^5}\]

Rest-to-rest check: \(a_3=10\Delta\theta/t_f^3,\ a_4=-15\Delta\theta/t_f^4,\ a_5=6\Delta\theta/t_f^5\) — the familiar 10–15–6 profile.

5. Linear Function with Parabolic Blends

Blend regions of duration \(t_b\) with constant acceleration remove the infinite-acceleration ends of pure linear interpolation:

\[\ddot\theta\,t_b^2-\ddot\theta\,t\,t_b+(\theta_f-\theta_0)=0,\qquad t_b=\frac{t}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{\ddot\theta^2t^2-4\ddot\theta(\theta_f-\theta_0)}}{2\ddot\theta},\qquad \ddot\theta\ge\frac{4(\theta_f-\theta_0)}{t^2}\]

Equality ⇒ linear portion shrinks to zero; larger \(\ddot\theta\) ⇒ shorter blends; infinite \(\ddot\theta\) ⇒ plain linear interpolation.

Example (Slide 31)

15° → 75° in 3 s, acceleration twice the minimum: \(\ddot\theta_{min}=4(60)/9=26.67\); \(\ddot\theta=53.33\); \(t_b=1.5-\sqrt{12800}/106.67=0.439\) s; \(t_{linear}=3-2(0.439)=2.122\) s; cruise velocity \(\dot\theta=\ddot\theta t_b\approx23.41^\circ\)/s. Distance check: \(5.14+49.68+5.14\approx60\) ✓.

Chapter 1 — Foundations of Neural Networks

RME 4221 · Perceptrons · MLPs · Activation functions · Losses · Forward propagation

1. McCulloch–Pitts and Hebb

MP unit fires if \(\sum_i w_i x_i \ge \theta\); implements AND/OR/NOT but cannot learn. Hebb (1949): simultaneous activity strengthens synapses. Together: architecture + adaptation rule.

2. The Perceptron

\(z=\mathbf w^\top \mathbf x+b\), step output; a linear classifier (hyperplane \(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x+b=0\)). Biological analogy table (dendrites, soma potential, all-or-none axon). XOR is not linearly separable ⇒ single layer fails ⇒ multilayer networks with nonlinear activations.

3. The MLP

\(\mathbf z^{(1)}=W^{(1)}\mathbf x+\mathbf b^{(1)},\ \mathbf a^{(1)}=f(\mathbf z^{(1)}),\ \hat y=W^{(2)}\mathbf a^{(1)}+b^{(2)}\); deep nets as nested composition \(F(\mathbf x)=f^{(L)}(\dots f^{(1)}(\mathbf x))\) giving hierarchical abstractions and making the chain rule natural.

4. Activation Functions

Without nonlinearity layers collapse to one affine map. Universal Approximation Theorem (Cybenko 1989; Hornik 1991): existence, not learnability or width; depth pays exponentially (Montúfar 2014; Telgarsky 2016). Sigmoid \(\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)\le0.25\); tanh zero-centred, \(1-\tanh^2\); ReLU \(\max(0,z)\), dead units; Leaky ReLU; softmax for multi-class. Comparison table; rule of thumb: ReLU hidden, sigmoid binary output, softmax multi-class.

5. Loss and Cost Functions

MSE \(\tfrac12(\hat y-y)^2\); BCE \(-[y\log\hat y+(1-y)\log(1-\hat y)]\); cost = mean over dataset. BCE punishes confident mistakes; with \(\tfrac12\) convention \(\partial L/\partial\hat y=(\hat y-y)\), without it a factor 2 (tracked consistently per derivation).

\(w \leftarrow w-\eta\,\partial L/\partial w\); sign logic; stationary points; loss surface vs training curve distinction.

3. Learning rate

Too small → crawl/local trap; just right → converge; too large → overshoot, oscillate, diverge; scheduling as practical compromise.

Conventions: \(L=(\hat y-y)^2\) (factor 2), BCE natural logs; \(\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)\); ReLU' indicator; sigmoid+BCE shortcut.

\(\hat y=0.6225, L=0.1425\); \(\partial L/\partial z=-0.1774\); update → \(L'\approx0.1135\); the ≤0.25 σ' factor per layer is vanishing gradients in one line.

Chapter 5 — Convolutional Neural Networks: Foundations

1. Why FC fails on images

1000×1000×3 → 3×10⁹ parameters in one dense layer; statistical and computational failure; ignores 2-D topology. Lineage: Hubel & Wiesel → Neocognitron → LeCun.

1–2. Sequential data and the vanilla RNN

Order matters; feed-forward nets have no state and fixed input size. RNN: \(h_t = \tanh(W_x x_t + W_h h_{t-1} + b)\); weight sharing across time; unrolling and BPTT; tanh for boundedness/zero-centering.

3. Task topologies

One-to-one; one-to-many (captioning); many-to-one (sentiment); many-to-many aligned (labeling); many-to-many unaligned (translation). Discriminating questions: is input a sequence? is output? aligned?

4. Vanishing gradients

\(\partial h_t/\partial h_{t-k} = \prod_j \text{diag}(\phi'(z_j)) W_h\); γ<1 vanishes, γ>1 explodes (Bengio et al., 1994); spectral radius and clipping (Pascanu et al., 2013); clipping cannot fix vanishing.

5–6. LSTM

Cell state conveyor belt; gates as sigmoid valves; \(f_t=\sigma(W_f[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_f)\) (added by Gers et al., 2000; positive bias init); \(i_t\), \(\tilde C_t=\tanh(\cdot)\); \(C_t=f_t\odot C_{t-1}+i_t\odot\tilde C_t\); \(o_t\), \(h_t=o_t\odot\tanh(C_t)\). Gradient path \(\partial C_t/\partial C_{t-1}\approx f_t\) — constant error carousel; ResNet analogy.

7. Variants

Peepholes (f,i see \(C_{t-1}\); o sees \(C_t\)); coupled gates \(C_t=f_t\odot C_{t-1}+(1-f_t)\odot\tilde C_t\); GRU (\(z_t, r_t, \tilde h_t, h_t=(1-z_t)\odot h_{t-1}+z_t\odot\tilde h_t\)); 3 vs 4 blocks; GRU ≈ LSTM empirically (Chung 2014; Greff 2017).

Three portrayals: robots want to be human (Bicentennial Man; Rachael); threat/Frankenstein Complex (Terminator, Cylons, Matrix, iRobot); superior robots being good (Demerzel; Astro Boy, Doraemon). Four human/robot similarity types. Science-scare and wacky-science stories; media guideline: stick to the study.

78. Ethics in HRI

Roboethics; robots as slaves and counter-movements; Asimov's three laws + zeroth law. Research: WoZ deception biasing expectations; persuasive robots (Brandstetter 2017; Kiesler 2008). Emotional needs: robotic care substitution argument; attachment and preference for robotic companions; asymmetrical bonds; persuasion vulnerability; abuse of public robots (Brščić 2015; Salvini 2010) and generalization concern. Workplace: job displacement/creation; education reluctance (Reich-Stiebert & Eyssel 2015: assistants yes, main teachers no); assistive home robots.

79. Conclusion

Cultural narratives shape perception and social-structural effects; researchers must be sensitive to them.

Question 1 — Interdisciplinary Design, the Uncanny Valley, and Design Method 12 marks

(as set): HRI project building a humanoid assistive robot with designers and social scientists: (a) divide the work and contrast ways of working; (b) decide the degree of anthropomorphism using the Uncanny Valley; (c) choose a design method and justify.

(b) Anthropomorphism

Uncanny valley: affinity rises then plunges just short of full human likeness (worse when moving). For daily home use: some human cues for trust and predictability; imperfect realism risky. Moderate, stylized anthropomorphism (Pepper-style), staying on the near side of the valley.

(c) Design method

Participatory design — users and caregivers as co-designers; constraints and lived experience cannot be anticipated; improves accessibility, acceptability, trust, adoption; accommodates the interdisciplinary team.
